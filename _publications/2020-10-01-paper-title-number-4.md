Urban greening is increasingly promoted as a remedy for urban heat, yet its cooling effectiveness and water requirements remain poorly constrained, particularly in arid regions. This study examines hydroclimatic and ecohydrologic controls on green space cooling efficiency (CE) in arid urban and peri-urban environments. We integrate publicly available spatiotemporal datasets -- including air and land surface temperatures, vegetation greenness (NDVI), canopy cover, precipitation, soil moisture, evapotranspiration, and near-surface meteorology -- in a case study of San Diego County, California. Using a conceptual framework grounded in surface energy balance theory and ecohydrologic process understanding, we apply a novel spatiotemporal sampling design to assess how vegetation, water availability, and atmospheric conditions interact to determine cooling outcomes and associated water consumption.

Our analysis shows that grasses and shrubs may provide CEs comparable to or greater than trees in areas with lower baseline greenness, while requiring less water. In contrast, trees maintain high CEs in areas with greater baseline greenness, but at higher water cost. We also observe that CE is modulated by soil moisture, suggesting that plant water stress may influence the effectiveness of greening interventions. We find substantial uncertainty in CE, with more consistent cooling observed only under relatively large increases in greenness. Finally, we provide policy-relevant CE and water demand estimates to inform urban planning. This research supports the understanding that evapotranspirative cooling plays a dominant role in green space cooling in arid regions, and highlights the need for site-specific, ecohydrologically informed strategies to support climate-resilient urban landscapes under intensifying heat and water scarcity.
